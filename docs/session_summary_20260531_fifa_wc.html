2. 802.1X Dynamic VLAN Assignment — Deep Dive

The Core Concept: One SSID, Many VLANs

You read correctly. Traditional design ties one SSID to one VLAN: Home_Team → VLAN 10, Visiting_Team → VLAN 20, Staff → VLAN 30. Three SSIDs, three beacons per AP, three times the overhead. At 10,000 APs this is significant airtime waste.

Dynamic VLAN assignment means a single SSID (e.g., WC_Secure) handles all authenticated users. After the client passes 802.1X, the RADIUS server returns three attributes in the Access-Accept message that tell the AP exactly which VLAN to place the client in — regardless of which SSID they joined.

The three RADIUS attributes (RFC 3580):

  • Tunnel-Type (64) = VLAN (13)
  • Tunnel-Medium-Type (65) = IEEE-802 (6)
  • Tunnel-Private-Group-ID (81) = "10" (VLAN ID as string, or VLAN name)

The AP reads these three attributes and places the client on VLAN 10 — no matter what SSID they connected to. Change attribute 81 to "20" and the next user ends up on VLAN 20. Complete isolation, one SSID.

Authentication Flow — Step by Step

  1. Client associates to WC_Secure SSID (WPA3-Enterprise or WPA2-Enterprise)
  2. AP (Authenticator) sends EAPOL-Start to client
  3. Client returns EAP-Response/Identity (username: j.smith@teamusa.org)
  4. AP forwards RADIUS Access-Request to EP1 Security via RadSec (TCP port 2083) with username + EAP method
  5. EP1 Security evaluates Network Policies — matches j.smith to User Group TeamUSA_WiFi → Policy_TeamUSA → VLAN 10
  6. EP1 Security sends Access-Accept with: Tunnel-Type=VLAN, Tunnel-Medium-Type=IEEE-802, Tunnel-Private-Group-ID=10
  7. AP assigns client to VLAN 10 and completes the 4-way WPA handshake
  8. A visiting team member (a.jones@teamaus.com) authenticates → group TeamAUS_WiFi → RADIUS returns VLAN 20 → complete isolation

5. Guest Security — OWE, Rogue Clients, and Ticketing Verification

Why OWE and Not Open / WPA2-PSK

Open WiFi (no encryption) means every over-the-air packet is readable by any device in range. At a 72,000-person event this is a serious credential theft risk, especially with CWP collecting emails and phone numbers.

WPA2-PSK with a published passphrase is functionally equivalent to open — anyone who knows the passphrase can decrypt all traffic.

OWE (RFC 8110 / 802.11-2020) provides per-client encryption with no passphrase. Each client negotiates a unique session key during association. An attacker who captures the air cannot decrypt any other client's traffic. The user experience is identical to open WiFi — no passphrase prompt.

Bottleneck Analysis — Where the Math Breaks Down

Scenario: 10,000 APs, busy-hour peak

AP5060 theoretical aggregate: 22.9 Gbps (Dual 5G/6G mode) or 18.5 Gbps (standard tri-band). Real-world stadium efficiency (overhead, contention, mixed clients): ~8–10% utilization → 1.5–2 Gbps aggregate per AP at true peak.

10,000 APs × 1 Gbps average = 10 Tbps theoretical aggregate. This does not exist as backhaul — nor does it need to. The internet uplinks are the binding constraint.

Critical bottleneck: Internet uplink. Two 100 GbE circuits = 200 Gbps total. At 72,000 simultaneous fans averaging 5 Mbps each = 360 Gbps demand — nearly 2× your uplink capacity. Resolution strategies:

  • CDN pre-positioning: Cache FIFA+ content (match replays, stats) at a local CDN PoP or on-premises appliance. Keeps most fan traffic local, off the WAN.
  • Traffic shaping: Per-client 25 Mbps cap on Guest VLAN reduces peak demand to ~72,000 × 2 Mbps average realistic = 144 Gbps — within your 200 Gbps budget.
  • Additional circuits: Work with CenturyLink/Lumen (the stadium naming rights holder has a relationship) for temporary 100 GbE burst capacity for the tournament window.
  • SATCOM supplement: Starlink Business can add ~500 Mbps per terminal. Deploying 10–20 terminals adds 5–10 Gbps — helpful for staff/POS VLANs but not a fan traffic solution at this scale.

Distribution-level bottleneck

At 10 GbE access switch uplinks with 24 APs per switch (each AP at 2.5 GbE), you have 60 Gbps of AP capacity feeding a 10 GbE uplink — a 6:1 oversubscription ratio. Acceptable for fan WiFi where simultaneous full utilization is rare. For Team VLANs (lower contention, higher criticality), ensure those switches have 25 GbE uplinks and <4:1 oversubscription.

PoE Budget

AP5060 power: 802.3at (PoE+) — 21W typical / 25.5W max (supports standard 2-radio operation). 802.3bt (PoE++) — 25W typical / 30W max (required for full quad-radio + all IoT radios). At 10,000 APs on 802.3bt: up to 300 kW total PoE load. Each PoE switch must have adequate power budget provisioned — do not rely on "budget sharing" modes that throttle AP radio power. Specify 802.3bt-capable switches for the stadium deployment. Work with the facilities/electrical team to confirm switch PSU capacity and UPS runtime for the distribution layer.

10. Disaster Recovery & Resilience

Failure Scenarios and Mitigations

Physical fiber cut (backhaul loss)

  • Primary: dual fiber paths into stadium from two physically diverse entry points (NFL stadium standard — confirm with facilities)
  • Failover: BGP pre-computed route; BFD detection <1s; failover to secondary ISP <3s
  • Emergency: SATCOM/Starlink activated for staff/POS VLANs; Guest internet service degraded gracefully
  • AP local mode: AP5060 supports local switching — if cloud connectivity to EP1 is lost, APs continue forwarding traffic using last-known policy. Clients already associated stay connected. New associations continue. Critical for match-day resilience.

PoE / power failure

  • UPS on all distribution switches: minimum 2-hour runtime (enough to cover a match)
  • Generator backup for stadium power: standard NFL/FIFA venue requirement
  • If a single access switch loses power: 24 APs go dark. EP1 AIOps detects within 60s and raises P1. Adjacent APs auto-increase transmit power (TPC) to partially compensate coverage gap.
  • AP5060 PoE++ (802.3bt) preferred over PoE+ for full radio performance — confirm switch PSU spec with infrastructure team.

Cyber threat / DDoS

  • EP1 AirDefense: always-on dedicated RF security radio — detects rogue APs, deauth floods, evil-twin attacks, RF jamming
  • Guest VLAN internet: upstream DDoS scrubbing via ISP (Lumen/CenturyLink has scrubbing services — activate for tournament window)
  • EP1 Security RADIUS: rate-limit authentication attempts per source MAC; lockout after 5 failed attempts (configured in EP1 Security → Policy → Network Policies → Advanced Auth settings)

  • Broadcast media RF: TV broadcast trucks use 5.8 GHz links. Coordinate with broadcast coordinator — their frequencies may overlap WiFi 5 GHz. Move sensitive APs to 6 GHz only.

11. P5G / 5G Coexistence & Handset Fallback

The Problem

Every fan with a cellular subscription carries a device that is simultaneously connected to both the stadium WiFi and the carrier 5G network. Modern handsets use traffic steering algorithms that continuously evaluate both interfaces and may route traffic to 5G even when WiFi is connected — or vice versa. In a dense stadium this creates two problems: (1) WiFi appears to perform well but much of the traffic is leaving via 5G anyway; (2) the P5G network inside the stadium (if EN is providing a private 5G overlay) must be coordinated with WiFi to avoid double-serving the same client.

Handset Behaviour: iOS vs Android

PlatformDefault behaviourWiFi preference trigger
iOS (iPhone)"WiFi Assist" — seamlessly uses cellular if WiFi is poor. Also uses "Multipath TCP" to bond both interfaces for some apps.Prefers WiFi if RSSI > -70 dBm and throughput is adequate. Strong WiFi signal = WiFi wins.
Android (varies by OEM)Most Android will stay on WiFi once connected unless quality drops below a threshold. Samsung "Intelligent WiFi" uses ML-based switching.Prefers the interface with higher estimated TCP throughput.

Fallback Mechanism Design

The brief asks: "what can be done to ensure there is a fallback mechanism?" The answer operates at multiple layers:

  1. WiFi quality above 5G threshold: If the WiFi network delivers >5 Mbps reliably, handsets will prefer it. Maintaining BSS Min Rate at 18 Mbps and the goodput targets in Section 6 keeps WiFi competitive with 5G.
  2. 802.11v BSS Transition Management: If an AP is overloaded, it sends a BSS Transition Request to the client suggesting a better AP — this keeps clients on WiFi rather than defecting to 5G.
  3. Hotspot 2.0 / Passpoint (802.11u): Enables authenticated WiFi offload from cellular. A carrier agreement with AT&T/Verizon/T-Mobile lets their subscribers auto-authenticate to WC_Guest using their SIM credentials (EAP-SIM/AKA). No CWP required; seamless handoff from 5G to WiFi and back. This is the stadium-grade solution for cellular/WiFi coexistence.
  4. ANQP (802.11u query): Devices broadcast "what networks are here?" before associating. EP1 responds with venue info, including roaming consortium identifiers that let carrier subscribers self-authenticate. Configure: EP1 → SSID → Hotspot 2.0 → enable, add carrier ANQP profiles.
  5. Graceful degradation: If WiFi becomes congested, the BSS Min Rate admission control (18 Mbps) prevents the AP from admitting clients it cannot serve — those clients fall back to 5G cleanly rather than being admitted and getting 1 Mbps on WiFi.

P5G coordination note for core engineers: If the stadium is deploying Extreme's Private 5G (P5G) alongside WiFi, the two systems must use a common policy plane. EP1 can manage both WiFi APs and P5G small cells from a single dashboard. The key decision: which clients are primary WiFi vs primary P5G? Recommendation: high-density fan areas → WiFi-7 primary (AP5060); outdoor perimeter / entry queues where AP density is lower → P5G primary. Flag this as an open architectural question for the EN pre-sales team at Lumen Field.

19. Identity Providers — AD, Local Users, and the Full Picture

Core insight: An Identity Provider is any system that answers "who is this user and what groups do they belong to?" EP1 Security doesn't care which one you use — the Network Policies stay identical regardless.

What Active Directory Is

AD is a directory service — a database of users, passwords, groups, and computers. Microsoft developed it; it runs on Windows Server. Every enterprise that uses Windows runs AD. Think of it as the corporate phonebook and security guard combined: it knows every user and every group, and answers authentication requests from other systems.

EP1 Security delegates credential validation entirely to AD. AD says yes or no and returns group membership. EP1 Security only makes the VLAN decision — it never stores the password.

20. QoE Optimisation — Radio Profile, Overhead Reduction & Live Monitoring

Objective: Maximise user-perceived quality (QoE) by eliminating overhead, forcing clients onto clean spectrum, and ensuring every microsecond of airtime carries useful data. These settings live in EP1 Core → Network Policies → Radio Profiles and are pushed to AP3000/AP5060 via the Network Policy.
