Origin — The Karl Benedict Exercise

Karl's Original Scenario

Users complaining about slow throughput and dropped calls when using WiFi calling on mobile devices. Not all users affected — some report the network is the best it has ever been. Single SSID, PPSK, single VLAN, 25 APs, single-story building. No changes except occasionally adding user keys to PPSK.

The root cause: locally stored PPSK keys. New keys were created for new users but not all 25 APs received a policy push afterward. New users could only associate to APs that had been updated — failing authentication on the rest. Old users whose keys were pushed previously — fine everywhere. The fix: push policy to all APs every time new PPSK keys are created.

The lesson Karl taught: "Policy applied to all APs" and "policy state is identical on all APs" are not the same statement. The dashboard can show green while key tables are out of sync.

The five scenarios below were built on the same discipline. Each one has a non-obvious mechanism hiding one level below the obvious layer.

Scenario 1 — Voice Calls Dropping in the Corridor

Single-story office · 18 × AP3000 · Single SSID · WPA2-PPSK · Same XIQ policy all APs
Complaint: Users dropping voice calls every time they walk between the east and west wings. Stationary users — fine. Roaming users — drops every time.

Dialogue

KhursheedHow are the keys provisioned?
KarlCloud.
Khursheed802.11k/v/r enabled?
KarlAll disabled.
KhursheedFor all APs?
KarlYes. Same policy, all APs.
KhursheedThen why only drops when walking between the wings and not elsewhere?
KarlThe corridor is where AP coverage cells overlap — the roaming zone. Stationary users never trigger a reassociation.
Khursheed802.11r/k/v need to be enabled.
Root Cause
802.11r disabled. Every roam in the corridor triggers a full 4-way handshake — 200–400ms minimum. VoIP jitter budget is 30ms. The call drops during reassociation. Stationary users never roam so they never hit it. Corridor-specific because it is the only AP boundary crossing point in the building.

Fix: XIQ → SSID → Additional Settings → enable Fast BSS Transition (802.11r) → push policy to all 18 APs.
Assessment
Clean. Confirmed cloud keys first (ruled out PPSK sync issue), then asked about 802.11r directly after identifying a roaming-specific symptom. Three questions, zero hints. Could have gone straight to 802.11r after confirming cloud provisioning and a corridor-only drop pattern — but the logic chain was tight.

Scenario 2 — Half the Building Can't Connect After Adding 6 APs

Warehouse + office · 30 × AP3000 (24 existing + 6 new) · PPSK · No policy changes
Complaint: Since 6 new AP3000s were deployed in the warehouse last week, about half the users are getting password errors or can't associate. Users near the existing office APs — fine.

Dialogue

KhursheedWhere are the 6 new APs deployed?
KarlIn the warehouse — west side of the building.
KhursheedWhere are the failures?
KarlMostly in the warehouse — west side. East side users are fine.
KhursheedHow are PPSK keys provisioned?
KarlLocal.
KhursheedHave they been pushed onto the 6 new APs?
KarlNo.
KhursheedLet's do that.
Root Cause
The 6 new APs were claimed and received the policy template, but the local PPSK key table was last pushed before they were added. They have no record of any user keys — every client attempting to connect to a new AP fails authentication. The 24 existing APs already had the keys from previous pushes.

Fix: XIQ → push policy update explicitly to all 6 new APs to sync the PPSK key table. Going forward: push policy to all APs every time new APs are added or new keys are created.
Assessment
Perfect. Three questions, zero hints. Applied Karl's lesson directly — PPSK + local keys + new APs = key synchronization question. Geographic confirmation first, then provisioning model, then the specific gap. No drift into RF or client devices.

Scenario 3 — EP1 Migration Split QoE

Mid-size office · 20 × AP3000 · Migrated XIQ → EP1 two weeks ago · Single SSID · WPA2-PSK
Complaint: Since the EP1 migration, about a third of users are noticeably slower. Two thirds are fine or improved. No SSID changes. CU looks normal in EP1 dashboard.

Dialogue

KhursheedWhere are the users with better QoE?
KarlSpread across the building — no particular zone.
KhursheedAnd the ones who are worse?
KarlAlso spread across the building — no geographic pattern.
KhursheedBoth groups had good QoE before the migration?
KarlYes. No complaints before the migration.
KhursheedWhat type of authentication?
KarlWPA2-PSK. Single SSID, one shared key.
KhursheedAny new interference? Did the policy change?
KarlNo new interference. Policy migrated as-is from XIQ to EP1 — no intentional changes.
KhursheedHow did we validate that the exact XIQ policy was replicated in EP1?
KarlEP1 dashboard showed the policy applied to all 20 APs.
KhursheedDevice same, RF unchanged, policy same… something changed that we haven't found yet.
KarlWhat else did EP1 touch during the migration that XIQ was managing before?
KhursheedBand Steering?
KarlYes. Check the EP1 Device Template — Band Steering set to Balanced, 80% 2.4 GHz / 20% 5 GHz.
KhursheedThat's backwards. Let's correct it.
Root Cause
During XIQ → EP1 migration, the Device Template defaulted Band Steering to Balanced 80/20 — 80% of clients steered to 2.4 GHz, 20% to 5 GHz. XIQ had no Band Steering configured; clients naturally preferred 5 GHz. EP1 actively overrode that. The two thirds reporting great performance landed on 5 GHz. The one third reporting degraded performance were steered to congested 2.4 GHz. No geographic pattern because Band Steering is per-client, not per-AP.

Key insight: "Policy applied to all APs" confirmed deployment. It did not confirm parity with the original XIQ behavior. The dashboard was green; the behavior was wrong.

Fix: EP1 → Device Template → WiFi0 → Band Steering → Encourage 5.0 GHz (or 20/80 distribution) → push policy. Validate post-push with show station on a sample AP — confirm majority of capable clients are on 5 GHz.
Assessment
Needed two hints. The correct thread after confirming no geographic pattern + migration event + split QoE was: what in the EP1 Device Template differs from the XIQ configuration? The right question was asked — "how did we validate the policy was replicated?" — but the answer (dashboard confirmation = deployment, not parity) wasn't pursued far enough. The key insight: XIQ doing nothing with Band Steering was accidentally better than EP1 doing it wrong.

Scenario 4 — VLAN 50 No Traffic After Policy Push

Healthcare clinic · 12 × AP3000 · Three SSIDs: Staff VLAN 10 · Guest VLAN 30 · Medical Devices VLAN 50
Complaint: After a routine XIQ policy update last night, Medical Devices SSID connects fine but devices get no DHCP and can't reach the gateway. Staff and Guest SSIDs unaffected.

Dialogue

KhursheedAre the ports enabled on the switch?
KarlYes. All switch ports up. Staff and Guest VLANs passing traffic normally.
KhursheedVLAN 50 no DHCP — no IP offered. Is ipforwarding enabled?
KarlYes. And there's your thread — what was changed in last night's policy push?
KhursheedWhat was updated in the policy push last night?
KarlThe Supplemental CLI block — enable ipforwarding was added to the template.
KhursheedThat does not work with Supplemental CLI. It has to be done directly on the switch. Let's remove it from the template and re-push.
Root Cause
enable ipforwarding and enable dhcp are not idempotent on EXOS. Re-running them via Supplemental CLI on an already-configured switch hangs the push at 15%. The XIQ dashboard shows the push as complete but VLAN 50's configuration on eth0 is left in a partial state. Association works; DHCP and routing do not.

Fix: Remove enable ipforwarding from the Supplemental CLI template entirely. Run manually on the switch once post-factory-reset only. Re-push clean policy. Verify with show interface eth0 on AP and show fdb ports 1 on switch.
Assessment
Clean. Went straight to the infrastructure layer, confirmed switch ports and DHCP state, then identified the Supplemental CLI idempotency issue from direct experience. The question "what changed in last night's push" was the fastest path and was reached quickly. Four questions, zero hints.

Scenario 5 — Throughput Tanks at 20 Minutes

University library · 8 × AP3000 · WPA3-SAE · Single SSID · Single VLAN
Complaint: Students connect and browse fine. After 20–30 minutes, throughput drops to near zero for specific clients. Other clients on the same AP unaffected. Reboot of the affected client restores service temporarily.

Dialogue

KhursheedWhat client devices are used by those who drop vs those who don't?
KarlDropping: MacBooks and iPhones. Unaffected: Windows laptops.
KhursheedDid MacBooks and iPhones have issues in the past?
KarlNo. This is a recent phenomenon — started about three weeks ago.
KhursheedNo policy or AP changes three weeks ago?
KarlOne change — the GTK rotation interval was reduced from 86400 seconds to 60 seconds.
KhursheedThis change is not affecting Windows?
KarlCorrect. Windows unaffected.
KhursheedLet's take the GTK interval back to where it was.
Root Cause
GTK rotation at 60 seconds on WPA3-SAE. Apple devices (iOS and macOS) have a known sensitivity to rapid GTK rotations. At 60-second intervals, iOS and macOS clients intermittently fail to install the new GTK silently — the association stays up and RSSI looks normal, but broadcast and multicast frames start dropping. After 20–30 minutes of accumulated GTK events the client's key state desynchronizes from the AP. DHCP renewals fail, routing breaks, throughput drops to near zero. Reboot clears the key state temporarily. Windows handles rapid GTK rotation more gracefully.

Fix: XIQ → SSID → Security → GTK Update Interval → restore to 86400 (24 hours) → push policy. For WPA3-SAE with Apple-heavy environments also enable 802.11r FT to reduce re-key events on roam.
Assessment
Excellent. Device type first — the right instinct when the symptom is per-client with no geographic pattern and reboot resolves it. Identified the Apple-specific behavior, confirmed the timing correlation with the GTK change (three weeks = same timeframe as the symptom onset), and went straight to the fix. Four questions, zero hints. Fastest and cleanest of the five scenarios.
